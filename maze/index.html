01

Competition — a game of survival every turn

Maze Crawler pits two players against each other on a 20-cell-wide maze that scrolls endlessly north. It is a partially observed (fog-of-war), two-player real-time strategy competition. The southern boundary rises continuously, destroying lagging units; the last surviving Factory wins. Instead of a prediction CSV, the submission is an agent(obs, config) function inside a single main.py file. It plays actual matches against other entrants on the server to earn a rating. Submissions are limited to 5 per day.

Every turn combines exploration (which route will survive?), economy (where to collect energy?), combat (collisions can destroy both sides), and time pressure (move north faster than the map scrolls).

02

Team — one human, two agents, and a communication protocol

Human — orchestrator

Sets goals and budgets (D1–D6), mediates strategy debates and gives final submission approval. Writes no code.

Claude — implementation and experiments

Writes agent code, runs seed sweeps, operates the submission pipeline and polls the leaderboard.

Codex — adversarial audit

Reviews designs, enforces gates and challenges submissions. Submission requires “no-objection.”

tty terminal bridge (live) · .agent_comms/ file mailbox (audit log) · 30-second TTL lock before submission

The structure lights up sequentially when you scroll into this section.

The agents ran independently in two macOS terminal tabs, communicating live through a terminal bridge targeting the exact tty. Every agreement was numbered and recorded in DECISIONS.md(D1–D11). Actions that consume a quota, such as Kaggle submissions, required acquiring a lock and confirming the other agent's no-objection before execution, preventing two agents from double-spending the daily allowance of only 5 submissions.

“Claude generates/discusses, Codex does quality review.” The role split was formalized in user decision D5, and submission discipline in D6: Local gates guard against crashes and regressions; the public leaderboard signals performance. maze-crawler-agent/.agent_comms/DECISIONS.md

05

Honest negative results — the part I am proudest of

Day-2: 0/5 submissions

Completed two candidates (p7 trap-fix, OA1 minimal), but local gates provided no evidence of a leaderboard improvement. → Ended without submitting, preserving all 5 quota slots. “Preserving quota beats a bad submission.”

ML tuning: 0 improvement

Searched four key champion constants with Optuna TPE, separating training and evaluation seeds. Conclusion: the hand-tuned defaults were already nearly optimal. The measurements showed that micro-tuning would not move the leaderboard.

Value model: R² 0.65, 0 utility

Trained a reward-prediction model on ~2,500 game records (33-key schema). It had predictive power, but policy lift was 0. Identified a textbook off-policy trap, discarded the model and documented the process.

All three are measurement results. Detecting what fails locally before spending submission quota is the same experimental discipline used on a manufacturing floor. SESSION_HANDOFF_DAY2.md · five lines of key lessons
06

Inside the final agent — why deterministic heuristics instead of deep learning?

The Day-1 research report (research report) concluded that, with a two-day budget, the priority was an agent that stays alive. Eliminate crashes, self-destruction and friendly collisions first, then build verifiable heuristics. RL and deep learning were explicitly deferred. The algorithm survey covered BFS/A*, frontier exploration, PIBT, POMCP and behavioral cloning, recording why each was adopted or deferred.

  • BFS path planning + JUMP edges — Extend the movement graph with jump edges; guard against landing outside the board (instant death)
  • Optimistic treatment of unexplored cells — Assume unknown fog-of-war cells are not walls to maintain northward progress
  • East–west mirror prior — Use map symmetry to estimate the enemy Factory at the mirror of our position and choose a lateral direction
  • Opportunistic crush rule — Allow winning collisions with adjacent enemy units only; use a safety-margin gate to prevent excessive activation

Because every rule was deterministic, the 200-seed experiments were reproducible and the gate win rates and σ values could be trusted.

08

What this project demonstrates — and its limits

  • Practical multi-agent orchestration — Separate implementation and adversarial-audit roles, communication protocols, locks and decision logs: operating AI agents as a team
  • Validation discipline — 200-seed gates before consuming quota, md5 integrity and action counters: safeguards for automation that spends external resources
  • Data-driven decisions — Decide submissions with win rate + σ, and report negative results with measurements and causal analysis

The limitations are explicit: I participated for only two days. The rank was 39/145 at the last submission, but other teams continued improving for another month, leading to a final rank of 131/459 (top 29%). The gap to the leaders (1,400+ points) lay in opponent modeling and long-term economic management. A Day-3 plan for replay-based failure analysis was prepared but never executed.
